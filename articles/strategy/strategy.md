# Establish an AI evaluation practice

Evaluations help teams assess an AI application's quality, safety, and
reliability during development and operation. They apply selected evaluators to
existing data or to responses generated by a model or agent. Results may include
metrics and, where supported, explanations; considered alongside evidence
coverage and release policy, they help teams diagnose problems, compare versions,
and inform release decisions. See the
[Foundry evaluation overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/concepts/evaluation-overview)
for the product workflow.

## Why invest in evaluations

Evaluations provide a number of benefits to teams building AI solutions:

* Establish a quality baseline from which the team can "hill climb"
* See whether an agent improves or regresses as fixes and features are introduced
* Helps to flesh out requirements early, especially for edge cases
* Moves the team from a reactive to a proactive posture
* Automates manual testing and removes guesswork, which scales as the solution complexity grows
* Speeds the process of adopting new models
* Encourages a Evaluation Driven Development mindset, with benefits similar to TDD 

Similar to application unit testing, the benefits for investing in evaluation testing increase as 
applications grow in complexity and scale over time.  Manual testing that was sufficient for an MVP can 
quickly become difficult to test at scale.

Evaluations are one part of the overall SDLC process. Combine them with
monitoring, A/B testing, and other signals for robust application management.
Because AI behavior can vary across runs, evaluations should account for that
nondeterminism; see [Interpret results and compare versions](../results/results.md)
for guidance on repeated trials and uncertainty.

## Start with business outcomes

Using our sample application as an example, Contoso Support, success is not simply “the agent seems helpful.” 
The customer needs an answer grounded in current policy, access only to their own order, 
and a real ticket when escalation is requested and confirmed. An agent's claim that a ticket exists is not proof of the ticketing outcome.

We define the outcome and the evidence to use in support of the outcome. Then assign owners for the outcome and who for acting on it:

| Outcome | Evidence to examine | Accountable decision |
|---|---|---|
| Useful answer | Business criteria, relevant context, human review | Product owner approves quality criteria |
| Correct order access | Authorization checks, tool results, observed effect | Engineering owner approves the access boundary |
| Completed escalation | Confirmed tool effect and ticket record | Application owner approves task behavior |
| Acceptable risk | Safety evaluation, adversarial findings, control tests | Safety owner accepts residual risk |
| Sustainable service | Operational failures, latency, full cost | Platform owner approves operating limits |

These are recommendations for the illustrative support scenario, not product
defaults.

## Definitions

- **Task (problem or test case):** A test with defined inputs and success criteria.
- **Trial:** One attempt at a task; repeat trials to understand variation.
- **Grader:** Logic that scores an aspect of performance. A task can use multiple
  graders, each with multiple checks.
- **Transcript (trace or trajectory):** The record of a trial's interactions,
  including available messages, tool calls, and results.
- **Outcome:** The final state in the environment, not just what the agent claims.
- **Evaluation harness:** Infrastructure that runs tasks, records evidence, applies
  graders, and summarizes results.
- **Agent harness (scaffold):** The system that lets a model act as an agent by
  processing inputs and coordinating tools.
- **Evaluation suite:** A collection of tasks that measure related capabilities
  or behaviors.

## Evaluation test types

Different dimensions can be used to categorize evaluations.  Microsoft Foundry 
identifies these categories:

* General purpose
* Textual similarity
* Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
* Risk and safety
* Agent
* Rubric
* Custom 

While Anthropic uses:
* Model
* Code
* Human

Or you could categorize by the application's architecure:
* Chat bot
* RAG
* Agent
* Translation

Or by evaluation capability:
* Turn
* Conversation

Regardless of how they are categorized, to get a comprehensive quality 
coverage you need to combine multiple evaluators. 

## A roadmap from no evaluations to a useful suite

This practical roadmap below is adapted from the methodology in Anthropic's
[“Demystifying evals for AI agents”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) for teams establishing an evaluation practice.  Start small and improve the evaluation alongside the agent.  

## Collect tasks for the initial eval dataset
### Step 0: Start early

Start small, the Pareto principle applies here.  Begin with a
manageable set of high-impact cases, not an arbitrary minimum test size. 
Write down intended behavior as product requirements and edge cases are being decided. 
Defining a task can expose different interpretations of “success” before 
they become inconsistent implementations.

### Step 1: Turn existing checks and failures into tasks

A task or test case is one test with defined inputs and success criteria. 
Start with what the team already checks manually before a release, common user
requests, and issues from feedback, support queues, or incident reviews. Prioritize
by customer impact and risk.  Convert recurring failures into cases so the suite reflects
real behavior.

### Step 2: Make tasks clear and solvable

Two knowledgeable reviewers should be able to understand and agree on what counts 
as success consistently. State the inputs, constraints, and
observable outcome. For example: after a customer confirms escalation, a ticket
record exists for that customer and issue; without confirmation, no ticket is
created. Check the persisted effect separately from the agent's final message.

A reference solution—a known-working result that passes the task's checks—can
help confirm that the task is solvable and the checks are configured correctly. 
If a valid solution fails, revise the task. If no trial succeeds, review the
task and graders before concluding that the agent cannot do it.

### Step 3: Balance the cases

Test both when a behavior should happen and when it should not to get a balanced
view of the application behavior. One-sided tests can reward overuse or underuse 
of a capability.

## Design the eval harness and graders
### Step 4: Make trials stable and outcomes observable

The agent harness, the runtime and tools that let the model act, is distinct from
the evaluation harness, which sets up tasks, records evidence, applies graders,
and summarizes results. It captures the transcript, including tool calls and
observations, and verifies important outcomes independently.

A **trial** is one attempt at a task. Keep the evaluation environment
stable and isolate trials from leftover state, shared data, or resource problems
where practical. Use a target and tools that represent the behavior being
evaluated, while preventing test runs from mutating real customer records.

### Step 5: Choose graders for the question

A **grader** is scoring logic for an aspect of the task. The article
[“Demystifying evals for AI agents”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
describes code-based, model-based, and human graders.  Cost and effort increases for each
cataegory.  Microsoft Foundry supports all 3 types.

Use deterministic checks where a condition is exact, such as whether a ticket
was created for the correct customer. Use a model-based grader when judgment
requires interpreting open-ended language. Use human review for expert
assessment, ambiguous cases, and calibration. A task can combine methods when
each provides distinct evidence.

💡A Microsoft [recommended strategy](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/concepts/evaluation-evaluators/rubric-evaluators)
is to use a rubric evaluator as the primary measure of agent quality. A rubric
evaluator is a not a single test, but is instead a **set** of criteria with **weights** 
to determine the relative importance for each test. Anthropic similarly recommends using
deterministic graders alongside of LLM graders.  

For model graders, use clear criteria, compare judgments with independent human
review, and revisit disagreements. Let a grader report that evidence is
insufficient rather than forcing a guess. Partial credit can help diagnose
progress on a multi-part task. Teams can choose binary, weighted, or hybrid
scoring according to the decision; the release policy must still define which
outcomes and evidence are required to pass. 

Avoid requiring a particular sequence of tool calls unless the sequence itself
is required by policy—for example, obtaining consent before an action. Grade the
intended outcome and necessary constraints, allowing valid alternative paths.

## Maintain and use the eval long-term
### Step 6: Read transcripts and grades

Review examples from successful and failed trials, not only aggregate scores.
Ask whether the agent made a genuine mistake, the task was ambiguous, the grader
rejected a valid result, or the environment prevented success. A failure should
have an understandable reason. Correct evaluation defects before using the score
to justify a product decision.

### Step 7: Watch for capability-suite saturation

Capability evaluations ask what the agent can do and where it should improve.
When the agent reliably passes the current capability tasks, those tasks may
stop providing a useful signal of further improvement. Keep useful cases as
regression evaluations—checks that
previously working behavior still works—and add reviewed tasks that reveal new
gaps. Do not treat either purpose as prescribing a universal pass threshold.

### Step 8: Maintain the suite as product work

An evaluation suite is a maintained set of tasks, graders, and evidence. Assign
owners for the infrastructure, task contributions, review, and retirement.
People closest to user needs and product requirements should help define what
success looks like. Practice evaluation-driven development: define intended
behavior in tasks, run evaluations during development, and improve the agent
against observed failures.

After release, compare production signals with the behaviors represented in the
suite. With approval, turn useful failures into development cases and refresh
held-out acceptance evidence through a versioned process. Keep historical
versions so earlier decisions remain interpretable.

## Combine evidence methods appropriately

Automated evaluations are one way to understand agent performance, not a
complete substitute for production monitoring, user feedback, A/B tests, manual
transcript review, or systematic human evaluation. Each offers a different
signal: automated tasks support repeatable checks before release; production
signals reveal real usage; people help interpret quality and disagreement.
Choose a combination that fits the decision and risk. This multi-method
recommendation follows the [article's section on combining methods for a
holistic understanding of agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents),
not a Foundry service requirement. The Foundry
[human evaluation workflow](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/observability/how-to/human-evaluation)
is one documented option for structured review.

## Set policy and fund the full loop

Before tuning, decide what is blocking, what is advisory, and what evidence is
required. Missing criteria, invalid results, or unknown candidate attribution
should not silently improve an average. Keep acceptance evidence separate from
data used to tune prompts and graders; once acceptance cases guide tuning, they
are no longer held out.

Budget for the complete practice: target and judge inference, optional
simulation, telemetry, storage, and human review. Set owners for trace approval,
redaction, dataset versions, retention, access, and deletion. Reduce cost with
risk-based cadence and targeted development suites, without dropping required
coverage. Follow the relevant Foundry
[evaluation permissions](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/observability/how-to/evaluation-permissions)
for the selected workflow.

### Leadership launch checklist

- The customer outcome, task success criteria, and unacceptable failures are clear.
- Product, engineering, safety, data, and platform owners know their decisions.
- Tasks are balanced, reviewable, and grounded in observable evidence.
- The team can distinguish an agent failure from a task, grader, or environment failure.
- Required coverage, thresholds, exceptions, and rollback authority are documented.
- Suite maintenance, evidence retention, access, and full operating cost have owners.
